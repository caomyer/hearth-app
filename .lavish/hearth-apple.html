Hearth for Mac and iPhone

A private, native app for your household's books. The Mac app comes first; the iPhone companion follows.

What it isA native Mac app first, an iPhone companion after it, for you, your wife and a few friends. Not an App Store product.
EngineBeancount with hearth on top, in Python, running inside the Mac app. Nothing is rewritten.
ScreensFully native, no Fava. Fava's income statement, balance sheet, transactions and holdings, with its click-through and filters, plus your Statements page and Review.
Getting data inYour existing hearth setup opens as it is: banks, mappings and history kept. New users connect a bank with their own Plaid keys, import statement files, or import a beancount ledger once.
iPhone, laterShows the books the Mac worked out and sends review decisions back. Only the Mac talks to banks.
Not in the first releaseThe iPhone app, a second household member, a query page, and documents.

Three risks: Python inside a signed Mac app is unproven for this project, so it is built first. Getting a Plaid account approved for real banks is the hardest step for anyone new. When the phone arrives, it is only as fresh as the Mac.

1 · How it fits together

Banks to Mac to iCloud to iPhone

The books Mac writes
After every recompute the Mac publishes what the phone needs to your private iCloud: transactions, balances, what is waiting in Review, and the Today figures. The phone only reads these.
Decisions phone writes
A review decision made on the phone is saved to iCloud as a request. The Mac applies it through hearth's own review rules, recomputes, and publishes again. Decisions made on the Mac apply at once.
On the phone, meanwhile
A decided transaction shows its new category immediately, marked "waiting for your Mac". Totals change when the Mac has recomputed, and say when that was.
Keys and tokens Mac only
Plaid keys and bank tokens stay in the Mac's Keychain, in the vault hearth already has. They never go to iCloud or the phone.
Offline
The phone opens and shows the last published books with no network. Decisions queue and go up later.
Freshness
Every screen that shows a balance says when the Mac last updated it.

The journey for each kind of person

New, wants it automatic

Has, or will make, a Plaid account
  1. Start new books, then Connect a bank.
  2. Paste Plaid keys. Once, ever.
  3. Sign in on the bank's own page.
  4. Two years of rows arrive, already booked and grouped. Each account opens at the balance the bank reports.
  5. Review opens on the biggest groups. A few dozen decisions cover most of two years.
Real net worth within minutes. Every day after: a handful of new rows.

New, no outside service

Will not use Plaid, or cannot yet
  1. Start new books, then Import a statement file or Enter a balance.
  2. Hearth names the account from the file and asks only what the file does not say.
  3. Rows are booked and grouped by merchant, since a file carries no bank category.
  4. Next month: drop the next file anywhere on the window. Overlapping rows are recognised and skipped.
Same app, same books. Connecting a bank later keeps everything.

Already keeps a beancount ledger

Fava users without hearth
  1. Import a beancount ledger. Hearth reads it and shows the check: entries, accounts, balance checks, net worth.
  2. Books open with the full history, the ledger's own accounts and categories, and nothing waiting in Review.
  3. Later, Add account, Connect a bank, and say which ledger account each one is.
  4. Only rows after each account's last entry arrive. From here it behaves like everyone else's.
The ledger comes in whole and can leave whole: export opens in Fava with the same balances.

Already runs hearth

You
  1. The app finds hearth's data on this Mac. Use this setup.
  2. Import your beancount ledger, once.
  3. Accounts are matched from the ledger. Confirm.
Same banks, same mappings, same history. Review holds only what is new.
3 · Transactions and categories

There is no import step

Transactions arrive on their own and are in the books at once. The only work left for a person is deciding.

1 · ARRIVE

From a bank or a file

A connected bank is asked each morning, when the app opens, and when you press the sync control in the toolbar. A file can be dropped anywhere on the window.

2 · MATCH

Never twice

A bank row is known by the bank's own id. A file row is matched on its content against what is already there; a near match is shown for a look, not silently dropped. Pending rows wait.

3 · BOOK

In the books, flagged

Every row is booked immediately against a suggested category and marked unreviewed. A card payment seen from both sides becomes one transfer, never spending and income at once.

4 · PROVE

Against the bank

The account's balance is checked against what the bank says. Agreement shows as a tick on the account. Disagreement is named, with the rows that make the difference.

5 · DECIDE

Review

New rows wait in Review. Until you look, totals count them at their suggestion and say how much rests on them.

Where a suggestion comes from, in order

1. Your own past decisions for the same merchant. "Dining twice before."
2. The two sides of a move between your accounts, paired into one transfer. Nothing to review.
3. The bank's own category, mapped to yours. "Bank says Restaurants."
4. Nothing. Then the row is asked plainly, grouped by merchant so one answer covers all of them.

The reason is always given in words, never as a confidence percentage.

What you can do to a row

Accept it, or its whole group, as suggested.
Change its category, including "New category…" typed on the spot.
Split it across categories.
Say where it went, when it moved to an account Hearth does not see.
Note or tag it.
Undo the last decision, whether one row or two hundred.

What the app remembers

A decision is kept with the transaction's content, so importing the same row again never asks twice. If the bank later changes the amount, the row comes back to Review and says so. Deciding a merchant once makes it the suggestion for that merchant from then on.

Default categories: short, flat, and never asked about in onboarding

Money in

PayInterestDividendsOther income

Money out

HousingGroceriesDiningTransportShoppingHealthTravelSubscriptionsUtilitiesFunTaxesFees and interestOther spending

Not categories

Moves between your accounts are transfers and are never spending or income. A refund goes back against the category it came from.

Edited in onboarding? No. Edited in place, always.

Nobody knows which categories they need before seeing their own rows, so a setup screen would be guesswork and a reason to stop. Instead:

At the moment of need: the category picker on any row ends in "New category…".
As a whole: Settings, Categories lets you rename, merge, nest one under another (Housing, then Rent and Repairs), and retire one. History follows the change.
With a ledger: the defaults are not used at all. The ledger's own accounts are the categories.

Each category also carries a kind: pay, investment income, other income, spending, or tax. Statements reads the kind, not the name, so it works on any chart of accounts. For a ledger the kinds are guessed from account names and anything unrecognised reads as "Other spending" or "Other income", so nothing is ever left out.

Everything is clickable, and one filter works everywhere

Fava's habit of clicking through and narrowing down carries over whole. The filter stays in the toolbar as you move between pages, and every page, statements included, recalculates under it.

Any figure
Opens the transactions that make it up. A cell in the income statement opens that category for that month.
Any account or category
Opens its page: balance or spending over time, and its transactions.
Any payee or tag
Filters to everything from that payee, or with that tag.
Any chart bar or point
Narrows the period to that month.
The filter
Time (a year, quarter, month or any range), account (one, or a whole branch), payee, tag, amount, and free text. They combine.

Back and forward as in a browser, and each filter is a token you remove with one click.

Review

How review works: (1) A transaction arrives already in the books at its suggested category, with an amber dot wherever it appears. (2) Review lists what is waiting, grouped so one decision covers many. (3) You accept the suggestion or pick another category; the dot goes and the transaction counts as checked. (4) Totals stop saying they include unreviewed transactions. (5) The decision is remembered for that merchant, and the last one can always be undone.

6 · Build plan

Five milestones, the riskiest piece first

The Apple side follows Porchlight: a generated Xcode project, tests on GitHub's Mac runners on every push, release from a script. The engine keeps hearth's existing tests.

  • The doorway: screens as data
  • CI green on GitHub

iPhone, then household

Repository
The apps live in the hearth repository, in an apple/ folder beside the Python engine, so a change can touch both.
Engine
Hearth's Python package with beancount 3 beneath it, started inside the Mac app, which asks it for each screen as plain data. A Swift rewrite would have meant porting about 30,000 lines of tested Python.
License
Beancount is GPL-2.0-only. That rules out a public App Store release and is fine for a private app whose source is shared with its few users.
Release
The Mac app handed over directly; the iPhone app by TestFlight internal testing or direct install.
Plaid
Each household uses its own Plaid keys, as in hearth today. Keys and bank tokens never leave the Mac. Anything that starts a new Plaid charge, such as reading holdings, is a separate switch with the cost stated.
Household, later
Hearth already has members, joint accounts and sharing rules. Your wife becomes a second member on the same Mac; a friend runs their own Mac and books.
