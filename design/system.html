Hearth design system

A financial tool a household reads often. Every value below is in hearth.css; this page gives the reason for each. Where a choice was close, the rejected option is named.

Principles

  1. The number is the interface. Type, colour and layout exist to make figures legible and comparable. Decoration that does not help read a number is cut.
  2. One hero per screen. The figure that matters most is the only thing at display size, readable in a second. Everything else steps down.
  3. Precision matches use. A net worth is read in dollars; a transaction is reconciled in cents. Showing cents on a seven-figure total is noise that slows the eye.
  4. Never colour alone. Every meaning carried by colour is also carried by a sign, a glyph or a word.
  5. Every absence looks different. Withheld, not yet there, loading, missing history and broken are five different situations with five different looks.
  6. Privacy is shown, not implied. Every private section says so, and every withheld figure says who withheld it.

Type

The platform's own sans (San Francisco, Segoe UI, Roboto). No web fonts: the app runs offline on the household machine, and system fonts have excellent tabular figures and a true minus. One family, three weights (400, 500, 600). Sizes are hand-set rather than a strict ratio, because the gaps that matter are between body, label and figure.

Colour and contrast

Warm neutrals carry almost everything. Colour is reserved for meaning, and each meaning has exactly one hue. Blue means "you can act on this" and never encodes data. Dark mode is its own set of steps, chosen and measured separately, not an inversion. Ratios are WCAG 2.2 contrast against the named background; AA needs 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for graphics and large text.

Identity

Each member keeps one hue on every screen: avatar ring, composition bar, legend dot. The three hues were run through a colour-vision-deficiency validator against every pair, in both modes: worst pair ΔE 9.2 (deutan) light, 9.4 dark, against a target of 8. Joint ownership is neutral grey: it belongs to no one member, and it keeps the three validated hues for people.

Member 1, blueMember 2, orangeMember 3, aquaJoint, neutral

Two limits, stated rather than hidden. Aqua is 2.8:1 on white, below the 3:1 graphics floor, so identity is always direct-labelled with the member's name and is never the only cue. A fourth member cannot get a fourth hue that passes the validator in both modes; they would get the neutral treatment with a label. See the weaknesses in rationale.md.

Rejected: a green brand colour, as in the approved mock. Green is the colour of "money in"; making it also the colour of buttons and headers would make gains indistinguishable from chrome.

Gain and loss

The most important distinction in the product, so it is carried four ways and survives any one of them failing.

In colour

  • Net worth▲Up $18,412 since Oct 1
  • Dining▲Up $196 over typical
  • Payroll+3,299.60
  • Corner Grocer−64.18

The same, with colour removed

  • Net worth▲ $18,412 since Oct 1
  • Dining▲ $196 over typical
  • Payroll+3,299.60
  • Corner Grocer−64.18
  1. Sign: "+" for money in, a true minus for money out. Always present, so the number alone is unambiguous.
  2. Arrow: direction of change, ▲ or ▼, announced to screen readers as "Up" or "Down".
  3. Words: every change names its comparison ("since Oct 1", "over typical"). "Up $196" on spending is bad news; the words say so.
  4. Colour last: green and red mean favourable and unfavourable, not up and down. Spending rising is red; net worth rising is green.

Spending is never painted red by default. Most transactions are spending, so red everywhere would mean nothing and would make an ordinary grocery run look like an alarm.

Space, radius, elevation

Space is a 4px grid: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 40, 48, 64. Panels pad 24 on desktop and 16 on a phone; table rows pad 12, which fits about fourteen transactions in a laptop viewport without feeling cramped.

Radius has three steps: 6px for controls, 10px for panels, round for tags and avatars. Small enough to stay businesslike, large enough not to look like a spreadsheet.

Elevation is flat. Panels are separated by a 1px line on a slightly darker page, not by shadows; stacked shadows on every card are the most common way dashboards become muddy. Shadow is reserved for things that float: the sticky top bar's blur and chart tooltips.

Motion

120ms for hover and focus, 180ms for switches and disclosures, one easing curve. Motion confirms that something responded; it never performs. Numbers never count up: an animated figure is unreadable for the second it matters most. Everything is cut to near zero for anyone who prefers reduced motion, including the loading shimmer.

Accessibility floor

  • Contrast: every text pairing in the table above passes AA; the lowest text pairing is 4.7:1.
  • Focus: one treatment everywhere, a 2px accent ring offset by 2px, shown for keyboard focus and never removed. Try Tab on any screen.
  • Keyboard: everything is a real link, button, input, select or disclosure. Switches are checkboxes. A skip link leads every page.
  • Semantics: tables are tables with captions, row headers and column headers; transaction days are row groups; charts are labelled images with a table twin one click away.
  • Colour is never alone: signs, arrows, glyphs and words carry every state (see Gain and loss).
  • Targets: 44px minimum on touch screens; 36px on desktop where a pointer is precise.
  • Live regions: import progress and "saved" confirmations are announced politely; nothing interrupts.

Components

Buttons

One primary per view. Secondary is outlined; quiet is text-only for low-stakes actions. Disabled buttons keep readable text and say why nearby ("1 of 3 confirmed").

Charts

  • A chart must answer a question a household asks. "Are we on track this month?" (cumulative pace against a typical band). "Where did it go, and what is unusual?" (category bars with a typical tick). "Are we richer because we saved or because markets rose?" (saved and market, stacked). If no question, no chart.
  • The finding is written above the chart, as a sentence with the number in it. The chart is the evidence.
  • Now is ink; history is grey. The current period is the darkest mark; everything else recedes. No rainbow series.
  • One axis. Two measures of different scale get two charts, never a second y-axis.
  • Thin marks, quiet grid. 2px lines, bars no wider than 18px, solid hairline gridlines, direct labels on the one value that matters.
  • Every chart has a table twin ("Show as a table") with the same numbers, for keyboards, screen readers and anyone who would rather read.
  • Privacy reaches into charts. A member who shares a total but not its parts appears as a hatched, labelled row; a chart that cannot include someone says so in its legend.
  • Rejected: donuts and pies (bad at comparing close values), sparklines without context, gauges and "financial health scores", and any chart that exists because dashboards have charts.
